Recovery after anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction is based on both time and function. The graft needs protection while the knee gradually regains full extension, flexion, muscle control, balance, and sport-specific capacity. Your surgeon’s protocol takes priority, especially when another structure was repaired at the same time.

## Phase 1: Protection and muscle reactivation (week 1)

Use two crutches and the prescribed knee brace. Weight bearing is usually advanced as tolerated unless your surgeon gives a different restriction.

The early range-of-motion target is approximately 0–90 degrees. Regaining full knee extension is especially important. Patellar mobilization may be taught to reduce stiffness and adhesion.

Early exercises commonly include:

- Ankle pumps to support circulation and reduce clot risk
- Quadriceps sets
- Gentle hamstring and gluteal isometric contractions
- Neuromuscular electrical stimulation when quadriceps activation is poor
- Straight-leg raises in the brace only when the knee can stay fully straight without a quadriceps lag

Practice a safe, even walking pattern on a level surface. Do not abandon the crutches while you are still limping.

## Phases 2–3: Normalize walking and begin early strengthening (weeks 2–4)

Progress from two crutches to one, then to walking without an aid only when swelling is controlled, the quadriceps can stabilize the knee, and gait is normal. Many patients reach full weight bearing without an aid around week 4, but the timing varies.

Flexion may progress from 90 toward 120 degrees. A stationary bicycle with very low resistance can help once the knee bends far enough for a smooth revolution.

Closed-chain exercises may begin in a protected range:

- Mini-squats from 0–30 degrees
- Leg press from 0–60 degrees
- Calf raises
- Supported single-leg standing and multidirectional balance work

Avoid twisting, running, jumping, and deep squats.

## Phases 3–4: Protect the graft while building strength (weeks 4–8)

Walk without crutches only after a normal gait has been established. Work toward full active knee motion, including terminal knee extension.

Strengthening may include squats, a protected-range leg press, lunges, calf raises, hip strengthening, and core control. Open-chain knee exercise may be introduced in a limited range when approved. Graft choice matters: a hamstring-tendon graft may require a more conservative hamstring-loading plan.

The knee should not become more swollen or painful later the same day or the next morning. If it does, reduce the exercise volume and discuss the response with your rehabilitation team.

## Phases 4–5: Intermediate strengthening (weeks 8–12)

The brace may be gradually discontinued between weeks 8 and 12 when your surgeon and therapist confirm adequate control. Progress closed-chain and core exercises, advanced hamstring work when appropriate, multidirectional balance training, cycling resistance, and treadmill walking.

A useful balance goal is more than 60 seconds of controlled single-leg stance. Quality matters: keep the pelvis level and the knee aligned over the foot.

## Phase 5: Advanced strengthening (month 3)

Continue lower-limb and trunk strengthening until side-to-side differences are small. Swimming may be permitted, but avoid breaststroke kicking unless specifically cleared. Outdoor cycling should begin only after safe mounting, emergency stopping, and road awareness are restored.

Before progressing, the patient should demonstrate a controlled single-leg squat to about 60 degrees without pain, knee collapse, or joint effusion.

## Phase 6: Return to activity (months 4–12)

Light straight-line jogging may begin around months 4–6 only after clinical clearance and adequate strength, motion, control, and absence of swelling. Agility and plyometric training should be introduced gradually, with close attention to landing and cutting mechanics.

Return-to-sport testing commonly includes quadriceps and hamstring strength, hop testing, movement quality, confidence, and sport-specific tasks. A limb symmetry index of at least 90% is a common minimum, but passing one number alone is not enough.

Rehabilitation usually continues for 9–12 months. Returning to pivoting sport too early increases reinjury risk even when pain has resolved.

## Key precautions

- Weeks 2–4 are a vulnerable period for the graft. Do not run, jump, twist, or squat deeper than instructed.
- Feeling better at month 3 does not mean that the graft is mature.
- Do not return to competitive sport solely because a certain month has been reached.
- Increasing swelling, loss of extension, repeated giving way, calf pain, fever, or wound changes require medical review.

## Summary

Successful ACL rehabilitation is a progressive process: first control swelling and regain extension, then normalize gait, rebuild strength and balance, and finally pass objective return-to-sport testing. The safest schedule is the one adjusted to your graft, associated injuries, examination findings, and sport.
