Valve repair, valve replacement, and transcatheter aortic valve implantation/replacement (TAVI/TAVR) can improve heart function, but strength and endurance do not return immediately. Older adults may also face frailty, balance problems, anemia, rhythm changes, and medication-related falls.

## Common terms

**TAVI/TAVR**
An artificial aortic valve is delivered through a blood vessel, often from the groin. There is usually no full sternotomy, but rhythm and vascular-access wounds still require monitoring.

**Open valve surgery**
The procedure may use a sternotomy. Sternum protection is commonly required for approximately 6–8 weeks.

**Cardiac rehabilitation**
A structured program that combines monitored aerobic exercise, strength and balance training, breathing exercise, medication education, and cardiovascular risk management.

**Borg rating of perceived exertion**
A 6–20 scale. An intensity around 11–13 is often described as light to somewhat hard and may be used when heart rate is less reliable.

## Phase 1: Hospital mobility and breathing (days 0–7)

Once blood pressure, rhythm, access sites, and tubes are stable, begin sitting, standing, bathroom walking, and short corridor walks with staff.

Exercises may include deep breathing, incentive spirometry, effective coughing, ankle pumps, seated marching, knee extension, and gentle shoulder and neck motion.

After TAVI/TAVR, monitor the groin for bleeding, expanding swelling, or increasing pain. After sternotomy, avoid forceful arm pushing and report pain, clicking, or drainage.

Stop activity for chest pain, severe breathlessness, dizziness, a markedly irregular heartbeat, or a significant blood-pressure drop.

## Phase 2: Early home recovery (weeks 1–4)

Walk three to five times per day, starting with 5–15 minutes and increasing gradually. Use a cane or walker when balance is uncertain.

TAVI/TAVR patients commonly avoid heavy lifting and soaking the access site for about one to two weeks, depending on wound instructions. Sternotomy patients usually continue protection for 6–8 weeks.

Add sit-to-stand, light leg strength, balance, and gentle scapular exercise. Keep effort light enough to talk in full sentences.

Families can help track weight, blood pressure, pulse, walking, fatigue, and symptoms. Anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication increases the importance of fall and bleeding prevention.

## Phase 3: Outpatient cardiac rehabilitation (weeks 4–12)

TAVI/TAVR patients may begin a supervised or home-based program around weeks 1–4 if the access site and heart status are stable. Open-surgery patients often begin fuller training around weeks 4–6 after wound and sternum review.

Aerobic options include walking, stationary cycling, or stepping, beginning around 20–30 minutes and progressing toward 30–45 minutes. A Borg score around 11–13 may be appropriate when approved.

Add sit-to-stand, stairs, calf raises, hip abduction, resistance-band rows, and light resistance. Warm up and cool down for 5–10 minutes, and avoid breath holding.

## Phase 4: Long-term activity and safety (after month 3)

Progress toward aerobic exercise three to five days per week and low- to moderate-intensity resistance exercise two to three days per week. Frail adults may divide exercise into 10–15 minute sessions.

Continue balance, gait, leg strength, nutrition, sleep, anemia, and medication management. Recovery from preoperative frailty may take 3–6 months or longer.

Mechanical valves, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, pulmonary hypertension, kidney disease, and pacemakers require a more individualized prescription. Never stop heart medication because you feel better.

## Warning signs

Seek urgent medical care for chest pain, breathlessness at rest, fainting, new neurologic symptoms, uncontrolled bleeding, an expanding groin swelling, severe palpitations, or rapidly worsening leg swelling. Contact the team for wound drainage, fever, sudden exercise intolerance, or weight gain with increasing breathlessness.

## Summary

Recovery progresses from safe early mobility to structured cardiac rehabilitation and lifelong exercise. Rhythm, wounds, sternum or vascular access, balance, frailty, and medication effects all shape the individual plan.
