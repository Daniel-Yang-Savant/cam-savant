Hip fracture recovery begins as soon as the patient is medically stable. Early mobilization reduces complications, but the allowed weight bearing depends on the fracture, fixation, bone quality, and surgeon’s order.

## Phase 1: Hospital recovery and getting out of bed (days 0–7)

Confirm the weight-bearing order before standing. Depending on the surgery, it may be weight bearing as tolerated, partial weight bearing, or a stricter restriction.

With staff assistance, practice rolling, sitting at the edge of the bed, standing, transferring to a chair, and short walks with a walker. Early goals include getting to the bathroom safely and avoiding prolonged bed rest.

Exercises commonly include:

- Ankle pumps and deep breathing
- Quadriceps and gluteal sets
- Heel slides and gentle knee extension
- Supported hip movement within the surgeon’s precautions
- Repeated sit-to-stand from a safe chair height

Pain control, hydration, nutrition, bowel care, and delirium prevention all affect rehabilitation. Family members can help by bringing glasses and hearing aids and reinforcing day–night orientation.

## Phase 2: Basic walking and self-care (weeks 1–6)

Continue the walker or prescribed aid until gait is safe. Practice dressing, bathing, toileting, and short household walks. Increase walking in several small sessions rather than one exhausting session.

Strengthening may include sit-to-stand, supported heel raises, standing hip abduction, knee extension, and low step practice. A therapist should check whether the patient can follow the weight-bearing rule reliably.

Home safety is essential:

- Remove loose rugs and floor clutter.
- Add night lighting and grab bars where appropriate.
- Use a firm chair with armrests.
- Keep a phone or call device within reach.

## Phase 3: Strength, balance, and gait (weeks 6–12)

After fracture and wound review, progress lower-limb resistance, step training, balance, and community walking. Work on hip abductors and extensors, which are important for pelvic control and fall prevention.

Do not discontinue the cane solely because pain is less. First demonstrate an even gait, safe turning, and adequate balance.

## Phase 4: Long-term recovery and fall prevention (months 3–12)

Continue strength and balance exercise at least two to three days per week. Walking, stationary cycling, and supervised group exercise may improve endurance and confidence.

Ask the medical team to assess osteoporosis, vitamin D and calcium intake, vision, footwear, blood-pressure drops, sedating medications, and previous falls. Preventing the next fracture is part of rehabilitation.

## When to seek medical help

Contact the medical team for increasing wound redness or drainage, fever, sudden inability to bear weight, a new shortening or rotation of the leg, calf swelling, chest pain, shortness of breath, repeated falls, or acute confusion.

## Summary

Recovery combines early supervised mobility, progressive leg strength and balance, safe use of walking aids, adequate nutrition, osteoporosis care, and practical fall prevention at home.
