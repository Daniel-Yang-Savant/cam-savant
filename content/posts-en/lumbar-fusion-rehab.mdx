Lumbar fusion recovery is slower than recovery from a simple decompression because the bone graft must form a stable fusion. The number of levels, surgical approach, bone quality, smoking, and other health conditions affect the schedule.

## Phase 1: Hospital and early protection (weeks 0–2)

Begin short, supervised walks as soon as medically stable. Use log rolling and keep the shoulders and pelvis moving together when getting in and out of bed.

Avoid bending, lifting, and twisting. Follow the surgeon’s lifting limit. If a brace is prescribed, learn when to wear it and how to check skin fit; do not assume that every fusion requires a brace.

Early exercise may include ankle pumps, breathing exercises, gentle abdominal bracing, heel slides, and frequent position changes. Do not stay in bed all day.

## Phase 2: Home walking and daily recovery (weeks 2–6)

Walk several times per day and add time gradually. Use a stable chair, avoid prolonged sitting, and alternate sitting, standing, and walking.

Practice safe self-care, stairs, showering, and light household tasks. Avoid vacuuming, heavy laundry, lifting from the floor, forceful pushing or pulling, and driving until cleared.

Continue gentle trunk activation without breath holding or visible spinal strain. The brace should not replace muscle use or safe movement habits.

## Phase 3: Core activation and gait strengthening (weeks 6–12)

After surgical follow-up, progress low-load trunk endurance, hip and leg strength, sit-to-stand, step-ups, resistance-band work, and longer aerobic exercise. Learn a hip hinge and neutral, controlled lifting pattern without forcing a rigid posture.

Do not chase flexibility at the fused level. Mobility work should usually focus on the hips and upper back while the lumbar region remains controlled.

## Phase 4: Strength, work, and community activity (months 3–6)

Progress resistance training, carrying, lifting mechanics, balance, and work-specific tasks. Return to work depends on the physical demands: desk work generally returns earlier than repeated lifting, vibration exposure, or heavy manual work.

Walking, stationary cycling, and swimming after wound healing are common conditioning choices. High-impact sport and heavy axial loading require evidence of adequate fusion and surgeon approval.

## Long-term bone and spine health (months 6–12 and beyond)

Bone fusion may continue maturing for many months. Avoid smoking and follow recommendations for protein, vitamin D, calcium, osteoporosis treatment, and diabetes control. Maintain regular strength and aerobic exercise.

## Warning signs

Contact the medical team for wound drainage, fever, severe new back pain, increasing leg weakness or numbness, loss of walking ability, or repeated falls. Seek emergency care for new bladder or bowel dysfunction, saddle numbness, chest pain, or shortness of breath.

## Summary

Protect the fusion early, walk frequently, and progress trunk and lower-limb strength after follow-up. The brace and activity schedule are individualized; healing quality matters more than reaching a date on the calendar.
