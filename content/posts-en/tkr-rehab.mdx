Rehabilitation after total knee replacement (TKR) aims to restore safe mobility and functional motion while protecting the surgical wound and artificial joint. Early swelling control and full knee extension are particularly important.

## Phase 1: Acute recovery (week 1)

Weight bearing is often allowed as tolerated with a walker or crutches. Follow any different restriction from your surgeon.

A common early motion goal is approximately 0–90 degrees. Work on terminal knee extension to reduce the risk of a persistent flexion contracture, together with assisted knee bending.

Early exercises may include:

- Ankle pumps
- Quadriceps sets
- Gluteal sets
- Seated knee bending and straightening
- Short, frequent walks with an appropriate aid

Use cold therapy and elevation as instructed. The heel should be supported so the knee can straighten; avoid resting a pillow only behind the knee for long periods.

## Phase 2: Motion and functional walking (weeks 2–4)

Progress from a walker to a cane and then to independent walking only when gait is safe and symmetrical. Do not rush to remove the aid if you still limp.

The motion goal is often 0–110 degrees or more. A stationary bicycle may begin with partial revolutions and progress to full revolutions.

Strengthening may include straight-leg raises, mini-squats in a protected range, sit-to-stand practice, and supported step-ups. Patellar and scar mobilization may be taught after the wound has healed.

## Phase 3: Strength and endurance (weeks 4–8)

Progress closed-chain resistance exercises such as leg press, wall slides, and sit-to-stand from gradually lower surfaces. Add single-leg balance with support, tandem walking, and reaching tasks.

Increase cycling resistance and build treadmill or level-ground walking toward 15–20 minutes. A useful milestone is a symmetrical gait and the ability to use stairs with minimal assistance.

## Phase 4: Advanced function (month 3)

Gradually return to community walking and low-impact recreation. Progress lunges, side-stepping, resistance-band exercise, hip strength, and balance.

A common functional motion target is approximately 0–120 degrees, although anatomy and preoperative stiffness affect the final range. Continue a home exercise program after formal therapy ends.

## Long-term recovery (months 4–6 and beyond)

Suitable activities commonly include walking, cycling, swimming, golf, and other low-impact exercise. High-impact running and jumping may increase implant stress and should be discussed with the surgeon.

Maintain a healthy body weight and continue quadriceps, hip, and balance training. Seek prompt medical review for a new hot, red, markedly swollen joint, fever, drainage, sudden instability, or escalating pain.

## Frequently asked questions

**When can I drive?**
Driving depends on which knee was operated on, medication use, reaction time, and the ability to brake forcefully. Obtain clearance from your surgeon; right-knee surgery often requires more time.

**Are clicking or metal sensations normal?**
Some patients notice clicking from the implant. It is often harmless when there is no pain, swelling, locking, or instability, but new or concerning symptoms should be assessed.

**How long does a knee replacement last?**
Modern implants often function for many years. Longevity depends on alignment, body weight, activity, infection prevention, bone quality, and implant factors.

## Summary

The early priorities are swelling control, full extension, safe walking, and wound care. Strength, balance, endurance, and low-impact activity then progress over several months. Use symptoms and professional assessment—not speed alone—to guide each step.
