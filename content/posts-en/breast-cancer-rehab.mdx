Breast cancer surgery can affect shoulder movement, chest-wall comfort, strength, sensation, posture, and lymphatic drainage. The type of breast surgery, lymph-node procedure, reconstruction, drains, radiotherapy, and complications determine the pace of rehabilitation.

## Phase 1: Protect the wound and drains (week 0–1)

Follow the surgeon’s instructions for drains, reconstruction, dressings, and arm movement. Keep drain tubing supported and avoid pulling it during transfers or dressing.

Begin relaxed breathing, hand opening and closing, wrist and elbow movement, gentle shoulder-blade setting, and short walks. Shoulder motion is usually introduced gradually rather than held completely still, but the permitted range varies.

Do not lift heavy objects, support body weight through the arm, or force stretching. New swelling, fever, drainage, or rapidly increasing redness requires review.

## Phase 2: Restore shoulder motion (weeks 1–6)

After medical clearance, progress assisted and active shoulder flexion, wall slides, gentle external rotation, chest-wall mobility, and scapular control. Aim to restore motion needed for grooming, dressing, and radiotherapy positioning without causing prolonged symptom flare.

Scar work may begin only after the incision is fully closed. Reconstruction-specific restrictions must continue.

Return to light household tasks gradually. Avoid carrying a heavy bag on the operated side during early recovery.

## Phase 3: Strength and fitness (weeks 6–12)

Add progressive resistance for the shoulder, upper back, arms, trunk, and legs. Start with a low load and increase slowly. Properly prescribed resistance exercise does not need to be avoided solely because lymph nodes were removed.

Build aerobic activity through walking, cycling, or another tolerated mode. Exercise can support fatigue management, sleep, mood, bone health, and physical function during cancer treatment.

## Lymphedema awareness

Watch for persistent swelling, heaviness, tightness, aching, reduced hand or arm flexibility, or changes in jewelry or clothing fit. These symptoms deserve early assessment.

Protect the skin, treat cuts and infections promptly, and maintain regular activity. Compression garments should be individually fitted when indicated; do not purchase tight compression without assessment.

## Phase 4: Long-term recovery

Continue shoulder mobility, progressive strength, and aerobic exercise. Gradually return to swimming, gym exercise, work, and recreation after wound and treatment clearance.

Radiotherapy may increase chest-wall tightness and fatigue, so stretching and exercise may need to continue long term.

## Warning signs

Seek medical review for fever, spreading redness, wound drainage, sudden arm swelling, shortness of breath, chest pain, severe new shoulder weakness, or symptoms of infection. A red, hot, swollen arm with fever needs prompt care.

## Summary

Early gentle movement helps prevent stiffness, while later progressive strength and aerobic exercise restore function. Lymphedema risk calls for awareness and early assessment—not permanent avoidance of the arm.
