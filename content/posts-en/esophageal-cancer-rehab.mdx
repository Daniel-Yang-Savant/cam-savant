Esophageal cancer treatment can affect nutrition, swallowing, breathing, posture, strength, and endurance. Rehabilitation is most effective when the surgical, oncology, nutrition, swallowing, and rehabilitation teams coordinate care.

## Before surgery: build reserve

When treatment timing permits, begin walking or cycling, sit-to-stand and lower-limb strengthening, breathing and coughing practice, and nutrition optimization. Smoking cessation and good oral care are important.

Report worsening swallowing difficulty, dehydration, or weight loss early. The dietitian may recommend texture modification, supplements, or feeding support before surgery.

## Phase 1: Hospital recovery

Early goals are respiratory protection, safe mobilization, wound care, and prevention of severe deconditioning. Sit up, stand, and walk with staff as soon as medically stable.

Use deep breathing, supported coughing or huffing, ankle pumps, seated exercise, and gentle shoulder and upper-back motion within tube and wound restrictions.

Food and fluid progression must follow the surgical team. Do not eat or drink before clearance, because the reconstructed esophagus and anastomosis need protection and swallowing safety may require assessment.

## Phase 2: Early recovery at home (weeks 1–6)

Eat the texture and portion size recommended by the team. Small, frequent meals are often easier than large meals. Remain upright during and after eating as instructed, chew thoroughly, and stop if coughing, choking, regurgitation, or severe discomfort occurs.

Walk several times per day and increase duration gradually. Add gentle posture, thoracic mobility, shoulder movement, and sit-to-stand exercise. Avoid heavy lifting and strain until the incision and surgical repair are cleared.

## Phase 3: Strength and cardiopulmonary recovery (weeks 6–12)

Progress aerobic exercise and whole-body resistance training according to nutrition, blood counts, treatment effects, and symptoms. Include hip and leg strength, trunk endurance, upper-back work, and functional stair practice.

Monitor body weight, hydration, fatigue, bowel changes, and meal tolerance. Persistent weakness may reflect inadequate intake, anemia, infection, or treatment effects rather than lack of effort.

## Long-term rehabilitation

Continue aerobic and strength exercise and follow nutrition guidance. Swallowing, voice, reflux, dumping symptoms, scar restriction, neuropathy, and return to work may need targeted care.

## Warning signs

Seek urgent medical advice for breathing difficulty, chest pain, fever, new severe upper abdominal or chest pain, significant bleeding, repeated vomiting, inability to keep fluids down, rapidly worsening swallowing, wound drainage, or signs of dehydration.

## Summary

Recovery requires more than walking. Respiratory care, safe nutrition and swallowing, posture, muscle strength, and endurance progress together and must be adjusted to the surgery and ongoing cancer treatment.
