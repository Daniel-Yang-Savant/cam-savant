Surgery for oral or head-and-neck cancer may affect swallowing, speech, jaw opening, shoulder movement, neck mobility, sensation, and lymphatic drainage. Rehabilitation should begin early and remain coordinated with the surgeon, speech-language pathologist, dietitian, dentist, oncology team, and rehabilitation professionals.

## Phase 1: Hospital recovery and airway protection

The early priorities are airway safety, wound and flap protection, secretion management, pain control, and safe mobilization. Feeding may temporarily use a nasogastric or gastrostomy tube.

Do not begin food, drink, or unsupervised swallowing exercise until the surgical team confirms that it is safe. A speech-language pathologist may assess swallowing clinically or with an instrumental study.

Begin short walks, ankle pumps, breathing exercise, and gentle limb movement when medically stable. Neck and shoulder motion must respect drains, vascular access, flap precautions, and the surgeon’s instructions.

## Phase 2: Swallowing, speech, and early neck–shoulder motion

Swallowing rehabilitation may include carefully selected tongue, lip, jaw, airway-protection, and effortful-swallow exercises. The correct exercise depends on the actual impairment; not every exercise is safe for every reconstruction.

After neck dissection, weakness of the trapezius from accessory-nerve involvement can cause shoulder droop and difficulty raising the arm. Early scapular setting, supported shoulder motion, and posture training may reduce stiffness without overloading healing tissue.

Begin jaw-opening exercise when cleared, especially before or during radiotherapy. Gentle, frequent practice is usually preferable to forceful stretching.

## Phase 3: Function during radiotherapy or systemic treatment

Continue swallowing and jaw exercise as tolerated. Radiation-related pain, dry mouth, mucositis, taste change, and fatigue can reduce intake and activity. Work with the oncology dietitian to maintain hydration, protein, and body weight.

Progress neck range, shoulder strength, walking, and light resistance exercise according to fatigue and blood counts. Exercise volume may need to decrease temporarily during intensive treatment.

## Managing head-and-neck lymphedema

Swelling under the chin, in the neck, or inside the mouth may occur after surgery or radiation. Assessment by a trained lymphedema therapist can guide skin care, positioning, compression when appropriate, exercise, and manual techniques. Do not massage a fresh wound, infected area, or region that has not been medically assessed.

## Long-term rehabilitation

Continue swallowing, jaw, shoulder, posture, strength, and aerobic exercise. Dental care, nutrition, hearing, voice, pain, sleep, mood, and return to work may all require follow-up.

## Warning signs

Seek urgent medical help for breathing difficulty, rapidly increasing neck swelling, significant bleeding, inability to manage saliva, choking with blue lips, fever with wound redness or drainage, or signs of dehydration. Report repeated coughing during meals, wet voice, unexplained chest infections, or continuing weight loss.

## Summary

Head-and-neck cancer rehabilitation is multidisciplinary. Swallowing safety comes first, followed by jaw, neck, shoulder, lymphedema, nutrition, and whole-body recovery tailored to the operation and cancer treatment.
