Combined rotator cuff and SLAP repair requires protection of two healing structures: the repaired cuff tendon and the superior labrum–biceps anchor. Progress is often slower than after a single procedure. Tear size, tissue quality, fixation, and the surgeon’s instructions determine the actual schedule.

## Phase 1: Maximum protection (weeks 0–6)

Wear the prescribed abduction sling continuously for approximately six weeks, removing it only as instructed for hygiene and exercise.

Shoulder motion is passive only. Typical early limits include external rotation from 0–30 degrees and elevation below 90 degrees. Do not force motion.

To protect the SLAP repair, avoid active elbow flexion and forearm supination. These actions load the long head of the biceps tendon and its repaired anchor.

Permitted exercises may include:

- Gentle pendulum exercise
- Active wrist and hand motion
- Scapular shrug and retraction without shoulder strain
- Elbow motion only within the surgeon’s biceps restrictions

Do not lift, push, pull, reach behind the back, support body weight through the operated arm, or sleep directly on that shoulder.

## Phase 2: Active-assisted motion (weeks 6–10)

Gradually discontinue the sling when approved. Begin active-assisted motion with a cane, table slide, or pulley. Work toward full passive motion before progressing active motion, and avoid fast or forceful overhead elevation.

Submaximal isometric exercise may begin with the arm in a neutral position, including gentle deltoid and rotator-cuff activation. No heavy lifting is allowed.

Continue avoiding resisted biceps contraction and heavy pulling. New night pain or sudden loss of function should be reported.

## Phase 3: Early strengthening (weeks 10–16)

Progress active shoulder motion in all directions without shrugging compensation. Strengthening may include light resistance-band external and internal rotation, scapular stabilization for the serratus anterior, rhomboids, and middle/lower trapezius, and controlled eccentric work.

Posterior-capsule stretching and gradual overhead reaching may be added when indicated. Light biceps strengthening may begin only after clearance.

Light daily activity is permitted, but avoid heavy lifting, sudden pulling, and uncontrolled lowering of the arm.

## Phase 4: Advanced strengthening (months 4–6 and beyond)

Progress resistance and neuromuscular control through diagonal movement patterns. Light throwing, catching, racket, or occupational tasks may be introduced gradually.

The involved shoulder should generally reach at least 80% of the other side’s strength before sport-specific loading begins. Repetitive heavy overhead work is usually avoided until at least month 6 and may require longer after a large tear.

## SLAP-specific restrictions at a glance

**Weeks 0–6**
No active elbow flexion or forearm supination.

**Weeks 6–10**
No resisted biceps work and no heavy lifting.

**Weeks 10–16**
Begin light biceps loading and gradual overhead activity when cleared.

**From month 4**
Introduce light throwing only after adequate motion, strength, and control.

## Key precautions

- Larger tears and poor tissue quality require slower progression.
- Avoid upper-trapezius shrugging as the arm is raised.
- Seek review for new night pain, a sudden pop, abrupt weakness, wound changes, numbness, or increasing pain after a specific incident.
- Do not test the repair by lifting something heavy just because daily pain has improved.

## Summary

Early protection is essential. Passive motion comes first, followed by assisted and active motion, controlled strengthening, and finally overhead or sport-specific loading after strength and movement criteria are met.
