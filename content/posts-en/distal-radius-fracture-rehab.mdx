Stiffness and weak grip are common after distal radius fracture surgery. Rehabilitation protects the fixation while maintaining finger motion, controlling swelling, and gradually restoring wrist and forearm function.

## Phase 1: Protection and swelling control (weeks 0–2)

Keep the splint and wound dry. Elevate the hand above heart level when resting. Move the fingers frequently through a comfortable range and gently open and close the hand to reduce swelling and tendon adhesion.

Also move the elbow and shoulder so they do not become stiff. Do not lift, push, pull, lean on the hand, or use the wrist to get out of a chair.

Check circulation and sensation. Increasing numbness, severe tightness under the splint, pale or blue fingers, uncontrolled pain, or difficulty moving the fingers requires prompt medical advice.

## Phase 2: Wrist motion and light function (weeks 2–6)

After wound review, a removable brace may replace the postoperative splint. Begin gentle active wrist flexion and extension, radial and ulnar deviation, and forearm pronation and supination if approved.

Use the hand for light activities such as eating, grooming, typing, and handling paper, but avoid carrying weight. Scar massage may begin after the incision is fully closed.

Exercise should create a mild stretch, not sharp pain. Short, frequent sessions are usually better than one forceful session.

## Phase 3: Grip and daily function (weeks 6–12)

Once imaging shows adequate healing, gradually reduce brace use and add light resistance. Exercises may include soft putty or sponge squeezing, wrist curls with a very light weight, forearm rotation with a short lever, pinch training, and functional grasp-and-release tasks.

Practice buttons, jars, utensils, writing, carrying light objects, and work-specific tasks. Progress load slowly because pain may lag behind tissue stress.

## Phase 4: Return to heavier activity (months 3–6)

Advance grip, wrist, forearm, elbow, and shoulder strength. Add weight bearing through a table or wall before progressing to the floor. Heavy manual work, falls-risk sport, racket sport, and contact activity require medical clearance and sufficient bone healing, motion, strength, and reaction ability.

Some stiffness or swelling may persist for several months, especially in older adults or after a complex fracture.

## Warning signs

Seek review for increasing redness, drainage, fever, worsening numbness, marked burning pain, unusual sweating or color change, severe swelling, tendon snapping, or sudden loss of thumb or finger movement.

## Summary

Maintain finger and upper-limb motion early, restore wrist movement gently after clearance, and delay resistance until bone healing is adequate. Daily function and grip strength often continue improving for many months.
