Leg pain may improve quickly after lumbar discectomy, but the incision, nerve, trunk muscles, and general conditioning still need time to recover. Numbness or weakness may improve more slowly than pain.

## Phase 1: Protect the wound and establish a walking habit (weeks 0–2)

Take several short walks each day rather than remaining in bed or attempting one long walk. Increase distance gradually while keeping symptoms stable.

Learn log rolling to get in and out of bed. Keep the trunk aligned and avoid repeated bending, forceful twisting, and lifting. Your surgeon may set a temporary lifting limit.

Early exercises may include ankle pumps, relaxed breathing, gentle abdominal bracing, and comfortable hip and knee movement. Change position regularly rather than sitting for a prolonged period.

Monitor the incision. Fever, drainage, increasing redness, a severe positional headache, or rapidly increasing back or leg pain needs medical review.

## Phase 2: Daily activity and basic core control (weeks 2–6)

Gradually increase walking time and resume light self-care and household activity. Break sitting into short intervals and use a supportive chair.

Rehabilitation may introduce:

- Abdominal bracing while breathing normally
- Pelvic neutral awareness
- Heel slides and supported marching
- Bridge progression when tolerated
- Hip and upper-back mobility without forcing the lumbar spine
- Sit-to-stand and hip-hinge practice

The goal is controlled movement, not maximal abdominal effort. Avoid breath holding, heavy lifting, high-impact exercise, and repeated loaded bending.

## Phase 3: Strength, endurance, and work activity (weeks 6–12)

After follow-up, progress trunk endurance, hip strength, squats or sit-to-stand, step work, resistance-band exercise, and longer aerobic activity. Practice lifting with a hip hinge and keep loads close to the body.

Office work may resume earlier with position changes and walking breaks. Manual work requires sufficient lifting tolerance, leg strength, endurance, and task-specific practice.

Temporary muscle soreness is different from recurrent radiating leg pain. Reduce the load and seek advice if symptoms travel farther down the leg, weakness increases, or numbness expands.

## Phase 4: Return to exercise and long-term prevention (months 3–6)

Progress walking, cycling, swimming after wound healing, resistance training, and work or sport tasks. There is no single “perfect posture”; the long-term goal is the ability to tolerate varied positions and loads with good control.

Maintain regular aerobic activity and trunk, hip, and leg strength. Increase only one variable—weight, repetitions, speed, or duration—at a time.

## Urgent warning signs

Seek emergency care for new loss of bladder or bowel control, numbness around the groin or saddle area, rapidly progressive leg weakness, inability to walk, or severe pain with fever. Calf swelling, chest pain, and shortness of breath also need urgent assessment.

## Summary

Walking begins early, while bending, twisting, and lifting return gradually. Core rehabilitation emphasizes breathing and control. Recurrent or progressive nerve symptoms should not be treated as ordinary exercise soreness.
