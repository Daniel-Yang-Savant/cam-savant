Temporary urinary leakage, frequency, fatigue, and reduced confidence are common after prostate cancer surgery. Pelvic-floor rehabilitation and progressive physical activity can support recovery, but catheter and wound precautions come first.

## Phase 1: Catheter period and hospital recovery (week 0–1)

The goals are to protect the wound and urinary connection, prevent clots and lung complications, and regain safe mobility. A catheter commonly remains for about one to two weeks, depending on the surgeon.

Do not perform forceful pelvic-floor contractions, hold urine, strain, or try to stop urine flow while the catheter is in place.

Practice ankle pumps, deep and diaphragmatic breathing, gentle limb movement, rolling, sitting, and short walks with the urine bag secured below bladder level. Avoid heavy lifting, straining for bowel movements, cycling, and vigorous exercise.

Contact the team if urine suddenly stops draining, blood in the urine markedly increases, or lower abdominal pain and fullness develop.

## Phase 2: After catheter removal (weeks 1–4)

Leakage is often most noticeable with standing, walking, coughing, and fatigue. Track pad use and leakage patterns without deliberately restricting fluids.

Begin pelvic-floor training after clearance. Imagine gently lifting around the anus and urethra without squeezing the buttocks, tensing the thighs, or holding your breath. Start in lying or sitting with a 3–5 second contraction followed by 6–10 seconds of full relaxation. Progress gradually and add short, quick contractions.

Use a pre-contraction before coughing, sneezing, standing, bending, or lifting a light object. Begin several 5–10 minute walks each day.

More exercise is not always better. Worsening leakage, pelvic pain, urgency, or lower-abdominal tension may mean the technique or dose needs adjustment.

## Phase 3: Pelvic-floor strength and fitness (weeks 4–12)

Progress pelvic-floor control into standing, stairs, squats, and walking. Add diaphragmatic breathing, pelvic-neutral control, bridging, sit-to-stand, light resistance-band exercise, and lower-limb strengthening.

Build toward 20–30 minutes of walking per day, continuously or in shorter sessions. If receiving androgen-deprivation therapy, resistance and aerobic exercise help address muscle loss, bone health, and fatigue.

Avoid breath holding and sudden heavy lifting. Exhale during effort. Do not reduce water intake excessively, because concentrated urine can worsen urgency and irritation.

## Phase 4: Long-term control and return to life (months 3–12)

Progress toward at least 150 minutes per week of moderate aerobic activity and two days of resistance training when medically appropriate. Adjust for cancer treatment, cardiovascular risk, anemia, and bone status.

If heavy leakage remains or progress is limited at 3–6 months, request urology and pelvic-floor rehabilitation review. Biofeedback, individualized exercise, medication, devices, or surgery may be appropriate depending on the type and severity of leakage.

## Frequently asked questions

**When can pelvic-floor exercise begin?**
Usually after catheter removal and medical clearance.

**How quickly does leakage improve?**
Recovery varies. Many patients improve over weeks to months, while some need longer or additional treatment.

**When can I return to cycling?**
Cycling places pressure on the perineum and should wait until wound healing and medical clearance. Begin with short, low-pressure sessions and an appropriate saddle setup.

## Urgent warning signs

Seek medical help for inability to pass urine, large blood clots, fever, worsening wound changes, severe pelvic or abdominal pain, calf swelling, chest pain, shortness of breath, or new marked leg weakness.

## Summary

After catheter removal, learn a correct pelvic-floor contraction and progress it into daily tasks. Combine this with walking, resistance exercise, adequate hydration, and timely assessment when leakage remains severe.
