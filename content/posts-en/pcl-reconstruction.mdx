Posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) rehabilitation differs from ACL rehabilitation. Exercises that pull the tibia backward can increase stress on the reconstructed ligament, so early knee flexion and hamstring loading must be controlled carefully.

## Phase 1: Acute protection (weeks 0–6)

Use the prescribed brace, commonly for 6–8 weeks. Avoid excessive knee flexion. Passive motion may be performed in prone or supported supine positions to reduce posterior sag of the tibia.

Many patients begin with non-weight bearing or partial weight bearing. Some protocols permit protected loading with the knee fully extended, depending on the repair and associated injuries.

Active hamstring exercise is usually prohibited during early healing because hamstring contraction pulls the tibia backward. The restriction may extend from 6 to 24 weeks; follow your surgeon’s exact instruction.

Early priorities include wound care, swelling control, quadriceps activation, ankle pumps, and safe transfers. A common motion goal is approximately 90 degrees of flexion by weeks 4–8.

Progress only when the wound is healed, pain and swelling are controlled, the brace is stable, and the knee has not developed increasing posterior laxity.

## Phase 2: Recovery (weeks 6–12)

Gradually increase motion toward approximately 120 degrees of flexion by week 12. Transition toward full weight bearing after week 6 as directed. Continue the brace until stability, swelling, and muscle control are satisfactory.

Emphasize quadriceps strengthening while avoiding exercises that create excessive posterior shear. Do not add open-chain knee extension or hamstring loading unless specifically cleared.

Progression goals include full weight bearing with a safe gait, improving motion, controlled swelling, and stable examination findings.

## Phase 3: Rebuilding strength and control (months 3–6)

Advance quadriceps and lower-limb co-contraction training. Add closed-chain exercises, balance work, and movement-control tasks in a protected range.

Examples include supported squats, step training, calf raises, hip strengthening, and progressive single-leg balance. Exercise selection should keep the tibia controlled and avoid posterior sag.

Before high-level activity, aim for near-symmetrical motion and a strength limb-symmetry index of at least 90%, together with stable single-leg control.

## Phase 4: Return to sport (months 6–12)

Introduce running, agility, jumping, and sport-specific work only after medical clearance. Functional testing may include single-leg hop, triple hop, crossover hop, strength testing, and patient-reported outcome measures.

Common return-to-sport targets include:

- Hop-test limb symmetry greater than 90%
- IKDC score greater than 90
- Lysholm score greater than 85
- No significant pain, usually VAS 2/10 or less
- Motion close to the uninjured side
- Stable landing, deceleration, and directional-change mechanics

Return commonly occurs between 6 and 12 months, but complex or multi-ligament injuries may take longer.

## Key precautions

- Do not begin hamstring strengthening early without explicit approval.
- Avoid letting the lower leg sag backward when the knee is bent.
- Increasing swelling, posterior instability, loss of motion, calf pain, wound drainage, or fever requires medical review.
- The brace, weight-bearing plan, and motion limits may be different when other ligaments or the meniscus were repaired.

## Summary

PCL rehabilitation protects the graft from posterior tibial stress, restores motion gradually, prioritizes quadriceps control, and uses objective functional testing before return to sport.
