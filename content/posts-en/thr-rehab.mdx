Rehabilitation after total hip replacement (THR) restores safe walking, hip strength, balance, and daily function. Precautions differ by surgical approach, so confirm which restrictions apply to you.

## Understand your hip precautions

**Posterior approach**

- Do not bend the hip beyond 90 degrees unless cleared.
- Do not rotate the operated leg inward.
- Do not cross the operated leg over the midline.

**Anterior approach**

- Avoid excessive hip extension.
- Avoid forceful external rotation of the operated leg.
- Hip-flexion restrictions are often less strict but still depend on the surgeon.

The surgeon may modify or remove these precautions based on the operation and your stability.

## Phase 1: Acute protection (week 1)

Many patients may bear weight as tolerated with a walker or crutches. Early supervised walking helps reduce complications such as venous thromboembolism and loss of strength.

Exercises may include ankle pumps, quadriceps sets, gluteal sets, and heel slides within the permitted range.

Learn safe transfers before discharge:

- Keep the operated leg controlled when getting in and out of bed.
- Use a firm, sufficiently high chair; avoid low sofas.
- A raised toilet seat may be required after a posterior approach.
- Use an abduction pillow if prescribed.

Do not pivot on the operated leg. Turn using small steps.

## Phase 2: Functional walking (weeks 2–4)

Progress from a walker to a cane as balance, pain, and hip control improve. The goal is an even gait without a trunk lean or pelvic drop. Continue the walking aid if removing it causes limping.

Within the prescribed precautions, begin standing hip abduction, gentle hip extension, mini-squats, terminal knee extension, and repeated sit-to-stand from a safe height.

Monitor the wound and swelling. New drainage, fever, increasing redness, or rapidly worsening pain needs medical review.

## Phase 3: Strength and balance (weeks 4–12)

Progress gluteal, hip-abductor, trunk, and lower-limb strengthening. Add step-ups, supported single-leg balance, side-stepping, and stationary cycling if motion and wound healing permit.

Practice stairs, community walking, dressing, and household activities without violating precautions. An occupational therapist can help with long-handled aids and home setup.

Before walking without a cane, you should be able to keep the pelvis level and walk without a limp.

## Phase 4: Return to daily and recreational activity (months 3–6)

Gradually increase walking distance and low-impact exercise such as cycling, swimming after the wound is fully healed, and controlled strength training. Golf and light hiking may be possible after medical clearance.

Running, jumping, deep loaded squats, and contact sport may not be appropriate for every implant. Discuss long-term activity with your surgeon.

## Preventing dislocation and falls

- Keep frequently used objects between waist and shoulder height during early recovery.
- Remove loose rugs and improve lighting at home.
- Use stable footwear and a walking aid when tired or outdoors.
- Do not combine bending, twisting, and crossing the legs.

## Summary

Know your surgical approach, follow the correct precautions, and progress walking aids only after gait quality improves. Long-term hip and balance training supports safe mobility and protects the new joint.
