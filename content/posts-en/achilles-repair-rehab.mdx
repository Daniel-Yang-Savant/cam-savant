The repaired Achilles tendon needs protection from sudden stretching while it heals. Boot position, heel wedges, weight bearing, and ankle-motion limits differ by surgical technique, so follow the surgeon’s protocol exactly.

## Phase 1: Protect the repair and control swelling (weeks 0–3)

Use the prescribed splint or walking boot with the ankle pointed downward. Weight bearing may be prohibited initially or allowed only in the boot, depending on the operation.

Elevate the leg, move the toes, and perform knee, hip, and core exercises that do not load the repair. Use crutches or a walker safely.

Do not actively pull the foot upward beyond the permitted angle, stretch the calf, walk barefoot, or remove heel wedges without instruction.

## Phase 2: Progressive loading and ankle motion in the boot (weeks 4–8)

Progress toward weight bearing in the boot as directed. Heel wedges are usually removed one at a time rather than all at once.

Begin gentle active ankle motion within the allowed range. Dorsiflexion is commonly limited to neutral during early healing. Avoid passive calf stretching and forceful resisted plantarflexion.

Maintain hip, knee, and trunk strength. A stationary bicycle may be possible with the boot if approved and mounting is safe.

## Phase 3: Transition out of the boot and restore gait (weeks 9–12)

Transition gradually to supportive shoes, often with a temporary heel lift. Do not leave the boot if walking becomes markedly uneven.

Practice heel-to-toe gait and begin controlled calf loading, typically from seated heel raises toward supported double-leg standing heel raises. Add balance and lower-limb alignment work.

Avoid sudden push-off, running, jumping, and aggressive calf stretching.

## Phase 4: Calf strength and single-leg control (months 3–6)

Progress from double-leg to assisted and then independent single-leg heel raises. Add step work, resistance, balance, and controlled eccentric lowering only when the tendon tolerates the load.

Monitor tendon pain, thickness, morning stiffness, and next-day response. A temporary mild ache may be acceptable, but increasing pain or loss of function means the load should be reduced.

## Return to running and sport (months 6–12)

Running starts only after clearance and adequate calf strength, ankle motion, balance, and walking mechanics. Useful milestones include repeated controlled single-leg heel raises, minimal side-to-side difference in calf endurance, no reactive swelling, and good single-leg squat and landing control.

Introduce straight-line jogging before acceleration, jumping, cutting, and sport-specific drills. Full return may take 9–12 months or longer.

## Warning signs

Seek urgent assessment for a new pop, sudden loss of push-off, a palpable gap, wound drainage, fever, severe calf swelling, chest pain, shortness of breath, or increasing numbness or color change in the foot.

## Summary

Protection from excessive dorsiflexion is critical early. Loading then progresses from the boot to normal gait, calf strength, single-leg function, running, and sport-specific activity according to healing and objective performance.
