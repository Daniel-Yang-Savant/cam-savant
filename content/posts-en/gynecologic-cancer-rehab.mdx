Gynecologic cancer surgery may affect abdominal and pelvic tissues, pelvic-floor function, leg lymphatic drainage, bowel and bladder habits, sexual health, strength, and fatigue. Rehabilitation must be coordinated with the oncology and surgical plan.

## Phase 1: Wound protection and early mobility (weeks 0–2)

Begin ankle pumps, deep breathing, gentle limb movement, and short supervised walks when medically stable. Practice rolling, getting out of bed, toileting, and coughing without excessive abdominal strain.

Avoid heavy lifting, breath holding, forceful abdominal exercise, and vaginal insertion until cleared. Follow restrictions related to open, laparoscopic, or robotic surgery and any lymph-node procedure.

Monitor bladder and bowel function. Hydration, pain control, and a plan to prevent constipation can reduce straining.

## Phase 2: Walking and lymphatic circulation (weeks 2–6)

Increase walking gradually in several short sessions. Add gentle ankle, knee, and hip exercise, calf raises, and sit-to-stand practice.

After lymph-node surgery, watch for persistent swelling, heaviness, tightness, aching, or changes in shoe and clothing fit. Exercise should be gradual. A lymphedema therapist can assess skin care, compression, positioning, and individualized movement.

Do not self-treat new swelling with forceful massage. First exclude infection, blood clot, cancer-related obstruction, and other causes.

## Phase 3: Pelvic floor, strength, and fatigue management (weeks 6–12)

After wound and pelvic clearance, rehabilitation may add pelvic-floor coordination, relaxed diaphragmatic breathing, trunk control, hip strength, resistance exercise, and longer aerobic activity.

Pelvic-floor exercise is not simply “squeeze harder.” Some patients need strengthening, while others need relaxation, pain management, scar treatment, or bowel and bladder retraining.

For cancer-related fatigue, use pacing but avoid complete inactivity. Start below your maximum, exercise regularly, and adjust around chemotherapy, radiotherapy, anemia, sleep, and nutrition.

## Phase 4: Long-term function and survivorship (months 3–12)

Progress toward regular aerobic exercise and resistance training. Continue monitoring for lymphedema, pelvic pain, urinary or bowel symptoms, sexual pain, neuropathy, and bone-health changes.

Return to work and household lifting gradually. Use breathing with exertion and increase load only when the abdomen, pelvis, and legs respond well.

## Warning signs

Seek urgent care for sudden one-sided leg swelling or pain, chest pain, shortness of breath, heavy bleeding, fever, wound drainage, severe abdominal or pelvic pain, inability to pass urine, or rapidly worsening weakness.

## Summary

Recovery combines safe walking, progressive strength, symptom-guided pelvic-floor care, fatigue management, and early assessment of leg swelling. The plan should change with the operation and cancer treatment.
