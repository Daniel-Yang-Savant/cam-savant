Lung cancer surgery may temporarily reduce breathing capacity, chest-wall movement, shoulder motion, and exercise tolerance. Prehabilitation and early postoperative rehabilitation can improve preparedness and help restore function.

## Before surgery: prehabilitation

If time and medical status permit, begin walking or cycling, lower-limb strengthening, breathing practice, and airway-clearance education before surgery. Stop smoking and follow the surgical team’s respiratory and medication instructions.

Practice diaphragmatic breathing, supported coughing, and use of an incentive spirometer if one is prescribed. Learn how to support the incision with a pillow.

## Phase 1: Hospital recovery (days 0–7)

Pain control is essential for effective breathing and coughing. Sit out of bed, stand, and walk with supervision as soon as medically stable.

Common exercises include:

- Deep breathing and thoracic expansion
- Supported huffing or coughing
- Ankle pumps and seated leg exercise
- Gentle shoulder elevation and scapular motion within drain and wound restrictions
- Several short corridor walks each day

Chest tubes, oxygen, blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation may guide activity. Do not remove oxygen or alter flow without instruction.

## Phase 2: Early recovery at home (weeks 1–4)

Walk in short intervals and add time gradually. Continue breathing and airway-clearance exercises when secretions are present. Perform gentle chest, upper-back, and shoulder mobility to reduce guarding.

Fatigue and breathlessness are expected, but activity should remain at a level that allows conversation. Use pacing: alternate activity and rest, and divide household tasks into smaller steps.

## Phase 3: Rebuild endurance and strength (weeks 4–12)

Progress aerobic exercise toward 20–30 minutes, either continuously or in intervals. Add sit-to-stand, step-ups, calf raises, hip strengthening, resistance-band rows, and light upper-limb strengthening after wound healing.

Some patients benefit from formal pulmonary or cancer rehabilitation, especially when chronic lung disease, frailty, chemotherapy, or persistent breathlessness is present.

## Long-term recovery

Aim for regular aerobic and resistance exercise adjusted to lung function, cancer treatment, anemia, neuropathy, and fatigue. Continue posture and shoulder work if the incision or guarding restricts movement.

## Warning signs

Seek urgent help for sudden or severe shortness of breath, chest pain, coughing up a large amount of blood, blue lips, fainting, or rapidly falling oxygen saturation. Contact the team for fever, wound drainage, increasing productive cough, calf swelling, or breathlessness that is worsening rather than gradually improving.

## Summary

Early mobility, effective breathing and coughing, chest and shoulder movement, and progressive whole-body conditioning are the foundations of recovery after lung cancer surgery.
