After ankle fracture fixation, bone healing and surgical stability determine when the foot may take weight. Do not use a generic calendar in place of the surgeon’s written weight-bearing order.

## Phase 1: Protect the wound and move safely (weeks 0–2)

Keep the splint or cast dry and do not put weight through the operated foot unless explicitly permitted. Elevate the leg above heart level when possible to control swelling.

Use crutches, a walker, or another prescribed aid. Practice safe transfers and stairs before discharge. Early exercises may include toe movement, gentle knee and hip motion, quadriceps and gluteal sets, and ankle pumps only if the ankle is not immobilized and the surgeon permits them.

Seek urgent advice if the toes become increasingly numb, cold, blue, very swollen, or difficult to move, or if pain remains severe despite medication and elevation.

## Phase 2: Restore ankle motion under protection (weeks 2–6)

After wound review and transition to a removable boot, begin gentle ankle motion if approved. Common exercises include plantarflexion and dorsiflexion, ankle alphabet, toe flexion and extension, and scar care after the incision is fully closed.

Avoid forceful stretching, twisting, side-to-side stress, and unsupported weight bearing. Continue hip, knee, and core exercise to reduce deconditioning.

## Phase 3: Gradual weight bearing and gait recovery (weeks 6–12)

X-ray findings and clinical examination guide loading. Progress may move from partial weight bearing in the boot to full weight bearing, then from two crutches to one and finally no aid.

Practice heel-to-toe walking without shortening the step. Do not remove the boot or crutches while you still limp or swelling rises sharply after activity.

Strengthening may include resistance-band ankle work, seated then standing calf raises, mini-squats, step-ups, and supported balance.

For stairs, use the handrail. Early on, step up with the stronger leg first and step down with the operated leg and walking aid first. Progress to alternating steps only when strength and control are sufficient.

## Phase 4: Strength, balance, and return to activity (months 3–6 and beyond)

Advance single-leg balance, calf strength, uneven-surface walking, and direction changes. Cycling and swimming may begin after wound healing and medical clearance. Running and jumping require near-full motion, good calf endurance, no significant swelling, and surgeon approval.

Swelling at the end of the day can persist for months. It should gradually improve and should not be accompanied by increasing redness, heat, severe pain, or loss of function.

## Red flags

Seek prompt medical review for wound drainage, fever, new deformity, loss of previously gained weight-bearing ability, severe calf pain or swelling, chest pain, shortness of breath, or progressive numbness or color change in the foot.

## Summary

Protect the fixation early, restore motion after clearance, and let imaging and gait quality guide weight-bearing progression. Calf strength and balance training remain important after ordinary walking becomes comfortable.
